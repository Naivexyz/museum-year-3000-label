One museum label from the year 3000, for an ordinary object from today: a plastic fork. A Node 22 program writes exactly that one line to `/output/entry.txt` — no trailing newline, at most 200 characters — for make.fun's "Museum of the Year 3000" bounty, where a judge model reads labels two at a time and picks the funnier one.

## Run it

```sh
node main.ts
```

To test locally without touching `/output`, point it at a scratch folder:

```sh
MAKEFUN_OUTPUT_DIR=/tmp/museum-3000-test node main.ts
```

## The entry

> Plastic fork (c. 2025). A single-use trident for eating. Used once, for about nine minutes, then buried for a thousand years. Archaeologists have found millions.

161 characters, one line, English, original.

## Why this line wins

Judged in the competition's own order:

1. **On-topic.** It reads exactly as a museum label: object, date, plain curatorial voice. A reader who has never seen a fork in use can follow every word of it.
2. **Funnier.** The joke is arithmetic, not wordplay: nine minutes of use against a thousand years underground. The contrast lands on the first read, with no setup to decode.
3. **Estrangement.** The future curator misreads the fork as a trident — a ceremonial weapon — and treats "used once, then buried" as a ritual. The misreading is the laugh, and it only works because the reader knows what a fork really is.
4. **Original and tight.** "Archaeologists have found millions" closes on a fact that is both plausible and damning: the punchline is the scale of the waste. No word is doing nothing; the line is 161 of the allowed 200 characters.

## Candidates that lost, and why

- **Parking meter:** "Parking meter (c. 2024). Citizens fed coins to a metal post for permission to leave their vehicles unattended. The post kept the coins. No one questioned this." — A fair misreading, but the absurdity is already visible to a modern reader; there is no surprising turn, and "no one questioned this" is a flat closer.
- **House key:** "House key (c. 2023). A small jagged wand that opened exactly one door. Lose it, and your entire home became a museum you could only admire through the window." — The meta-museum twist is clever, but "wand" is a familiar trope and the punchline needs a beat to land; it loses on first-read impact.
- **Group chat:** "Group chat (c. 2025). Forty-seven humans shared one endless conversation. Forty-six only read it. The forty-seventh typed 'lol' and was considered the life of the party." — Funny, but the "lol" gag is a well-worn group-chat joke, and the 47/46 numbers invite nitpicking instead of laughter.
- **Plastic fork (alt):** "Plastic fork (c. 2025). Humans ate with four tiny spears, then threw the spears away. Every meal required a new weapon. They called this progress." — Good misreading, but "they called this progress" is a tired closer; "found millions" ends harder.
- **Parking meter (alt):** "Parking meter (c. 2024). A coin-eating post that punished cars. Humans obeyed it without question, though it had no arms, no eyes, and no mercy." — Punchy, but it repeats the first parking-meter joke's premise; the fork's time-scale contrast is more original.
